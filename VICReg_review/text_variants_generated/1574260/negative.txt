*My Friendly Neighborhood* presents a premise that is more unsettling in concept than it is in execution: a once-popular Saturday morning puppet show is revived years after its studio shuts down, and the result is a hostile, off-kilter survival horror scenario built around puppets that are far from friendly. The game follows Handyman Gordon as he investigates the studio’s reawakening broadcast and discovers that the neighborhood has become dangerous, deceptive, and increasingly absurd.

Mechanically, it leans into the familiar structure of survival horror without reinventing it. Players are pushed through non-linear exploration, puzzle-solving, and combat encounters against puppet adversaries that can be fought, but not permanently removed in the usual way. The game emphasizes map use, inventory management, and resource awareness through a grid-based real-time inventory system. Its weapon selection is intentionally odd, including tools like the Rolodexer, with ammunition tied to ABCs rather than conventional firearm logic. Puzzles are a major part of progression, and the environments range from backstage areas like Ray’s Workshop to surreal spaces such as cardboard crayon halls.

The biggest problem is that the game seems determined to be “different” without offering much beyond that novelty. It relies on the contrast between colorful puppet aesthetics and horror gameplay, but the underlying structure remains rooted in standard survival horror conventions. The advertised lack of gore is presented as a virtue, yet it also leaves the scares dependent on atmosphere and gimmickry, which may not be enough for players wanting stronger tension or more substantive danger. Even the promise that puppets “don’t stay down for good” feels more like a mechanical workaround than a compelling horror idea.

The included post-game Neighborhorde mode suggests extra replay value, but it does little to change the impression that the game’s appeal is highly specific. *My Friendly Neighborhood* clearly aims at horror fans looking for something unusual, but its quirks can come across as forced, and its deliberately playful tone risks undercutting the fear it is trying to generate.
