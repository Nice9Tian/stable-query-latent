*Star Wars: Empire at War* and its expansion *Forces of Corruption* are presented as a grand strategy package, but the reality behind the pitch is a fairly blunt exercise in commanding familiar Star Wars factions through a very conventional war game structure. You can choose the Rebellion, the Empire, or the criminal underworld, then direct troops, starships, and even the Death Star across ground, space, and galactic campaigns. It sounds expansive, but much of the appeal is just the license doing the heavy lifting.

The base game puts you in charge of battles across more than 80 ground and space locations, including Kashyyyk, Tatooine, and Dagobah. You are told to steer your side to victory through decisions that affect future battles and the overall fate of the galaxy. In practice, this means juggling combat on multiple fronts with a focus on Star Wars spectacle rather than deeper strategy. The game also advertises that you do not need to bother with “tedious resource gathering,” which may sound convenient, but also suggests a simplified loop that strips away some of the usual strategic texture.

Familiar characters like Obi-Wan Kenobi, Luke Skywalker, Darth Vader, and Boba Fett are available to command, and classic matchups such as X-wings versus TIE fighters are central to the experience. You can send in fleet actions in space and then land forces to take control of planets below. The idea is clearly to stage large-scale Star Wars conflicts, but the description leans heavily on famous icons and set pieces instead of demonstrating any especially original design.

*Forces of Corruption* adds a darker criminal angle through Tyber Zann, a would-be underworld ruler who uses piracy, kidnapping, racketeering, and bribery to seize power. It introduces over 28 new underworld units, along with new Rebel and Imperial forces and heroes, spread across 13 new planets. There is also expanded multiplayer, including three-faction battles and mirror play, where the same side can be played against itself. Even with these additions, the expansion still feels like more of the same, just with a slightly different coat of corruption over an already familiar framework.

Overall, this collection promises the chance to command or corrupt an entire galaxy, but it mostly relies on the Star Wars setting to make a standard strategy game sound larger and more ambitious than it really is.
