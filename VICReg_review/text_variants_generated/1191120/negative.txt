Growing Up sells itself as a life-simulation coming-of-age game, but the result sounds less like a coherent experience and more like a checklist of features trying to disguise a very familiar concept. You are supposedly in control of your childhood, adulthood, and even parenthood, making choices about what you learn, who you befriend, and who you fall in love with, while the cycle repeats through your child’s life. In theory, that premise could be interesting, but the page leans so heavily on broad promises that it feels more like marketing gloss than a clearly defined game.

The setting is framed as the 1990s, with more than 30 locations to explore, from cinemas to street shops, all wrapped in nostalgic scenery and pop-culture references. That might sound charming on paper, yet the description gives the impression of a static backdrop rather than a living world. The city is said to be “not going anywhere,” which is hardly a compelling pitch for exploration. Instead of suggesting a rich environment, it comes across as a mildly decorative stage for routine life management.

Gameplay appears to revolve around managing daily schedules, taking part in mini-games, attending school exams, and building up skills through extracurricular activities. There is also a “handcrafted mini-game” for developing mind and aptitude, though the page does not make it sound especially distinctive. The game boasts over 200 skills and 42 careers, ranging from astronaut to CEO to President of the United States, but sheer quantity does not automatically translate to meaningful depth. Without stronger evidence of how these systems interact, it reads like inflated variety rather than genuine complexity.

Relationships are another major selling point, with 19 characters whose stories supposedly unfold alongside yours. You can pursue them romantically or platonically, and the branching narrative promises more than 1000 lines of dialogue per character along with multiple endings. Again, the phrasing is ambitious, but also vague. A game can advertise branching paths and meaningful consequences all day long; that does not guarantee the writing is sharp, the outcomes are satisfying, or the choices are actually as impactful as claimed.

Customization is included through clothing, hairstyles, and room appearance tied to the skills you learn, while your choices can also unlock new locations, characters, and features for a future offspring. The idea of passing advantages down to the next generation is one of the more interesting parts of the concept, but even that is presented more as a bullet point than a carefully developed mechanic. The game seems determined to promise “unique playthroughs” and “dynamic” experiences, yet the description never fully escapes the sense that it is assembling standard life-sim components under a nostalgic wrapper.

The store page also highlights an “all-star team” of creators, listing names associated with other games, comics, and writing projects. That kind of pedigree may sound impressive, but it does not change the fact that the page itself is mostly a long list of ambitions: lots of skills, lots of careers, lots of characters, lots of choices, lots of endings. Growing Up appears to be trying very hard to look deep, but from the information provided, it comes off as overpromising and underexplaining.
