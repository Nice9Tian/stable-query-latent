Sub Rosa presents itself as a multiplayer first-person shooter built around tense deals, double-crosses, and the occasional high-speed car chase, but that premise is not enough to hide how rough and cautious any recommendation must be. The game is still in an extended and indefinite Early Access period, and the page itself openly warns players not to buy it without watching gameplay or doing at least a modest amount of research first. That alone says a lot.

Its current structure is split into three modes, though none of them sound especially reassuring if you are expecting a polished or settled experience. In World Mode, multiple corporations compete for money and power by controlling colored disks through either clean trades or violent takeovers. Cash and disks are both important resources, used to buy guns, cars, equipment, and clothing. The mode revolves around making deals, handling transactions, and, when diplomacy fails, shooting your way to advantage. The idea is that smart business practices can lead to wealth, while a steady aim and quick trigger can get you there faster if you survive long enough.

Round Mode is basically a round-based version of the same corporate conflict. It runs over a five-day work week, and the total money a corporation accumulates across those days determines its standing at the end of the round. It does not sound like a major reinvention so much as a more structured variant of the same core loop.

Eliminator Mode is the most role-driven of the three, but even here the setup feels more like a gimmick than a strong foundation. One player is secretly the Eliminator and must kill the player assigned as the Savior. The Savior does not initially know their role, while another player, the Protector, is informed and can periodically track the Savior. The rest of the players, including the Savior, are treated as Civilians and must either survive the Eliminator or bring them down with a grenade. It is a social deduction setup layered onto a shooter, though the page does not suggest anything beyond that basic structure.

Overall, Sub Rosa seems defined more by intrigue, betrayal, and improvised violence than by clarity or stability. The store page does not try to oversell it as finished, and that honesty is probably the most trustworthy part of the whole presentation.
