Across the Obelisk tries to sell itself as a roguelite deckbuilder with cooperative flair, but beneath the pitch it mostly sounds like a pile of recycled genre promises. It is available on Windows, Mac, Linux, Nintendo Switch, PlayStation 5, and Xbox Series X|S, and it supports English, Spanish, Japanese, Korean, Simplified Chinese, Traditional Chinese, German, and French. It also does not require a Paradox Account for normal play, though cross-platform co-op does use one for muting and reporting purposes. Story Packs can be shared across a party, but DLC heroes are not shared, which immediately undercuts the supposed generosity of the system.

The game centers on rescuing the king’s daughter from the treacherous Lord Hanshek before she dies, with the fate of Senenthia hanging in the balance. That setup sounds urgent, but the structure is clearly built around repetition rather than narrative strength. Players control a party of four heroes, either alone or in 2-4 player online co-op, and move through procedurally generated events, quests, and tactical battles. The game insists that choices matter, but it frames this in the most familiar possible way: save some people, leave others behind, and watch the story branch in the usual lightweight roguelite fashion.

Combat and progression are built around deck construction, but again the scale feels more like quantity than inspiration. Across the Obelisk boasts over 500 cards, more than 300 items, and 16 heroes divided into four classes. You can customize and upgrade decks with XP and perk points, combine different character classes, and try to discover new card-item-hero synergies on repeated runs. The game also offers four acts, each with a boss, plus random events along the journey. In practice, this means a structure that depends heavily on grinding out combinations and replaying variations of the same route.

There are three main game modes, though the description leans hardest on “obelisks mode” and weekly challenges. Obelisk mode is presented as a tougher roguelike experience where decks are drafted and the adventure is randomized, while weekly challenge mode fixes both the deck and map so success depends on skill. That sounds like variety, but it also exposes how much the experience rests on predefined systems rather than meaningful depth. Regular updates and content drops are promised as if they are supposed to compensate for the game’s endless repetition.

Co-op is the main selling point, yet even that comes with caveats. Crossplay is supported, and players can join friends on other platforms, but the need for a Paradox Account in that situation adds friction where there should be none. The idea of sharing Story DLCs across a party is convenient, but limiting hero sharing makes the feature feel incomplete. Across the Obelisk offers the usual mix of tactical combat, deckbuilding, and replayable runs, but it does so with so much emphasis on endless variety that it starts to sound like it is trying to distract from a lack of memorable substance.
