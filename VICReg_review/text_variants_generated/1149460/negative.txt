**ICARUS** presents itself as a PvE survival game, but much of what it offers feels like an exercise in attrition rather than genuine excitement. Set on a terraformed Earth-like planet that humanity already knows was a catastrophic mistake, the game drops players into a hostile wilderness where everything seems designed to drain time, patience, and resources. It supports mission-based play and a persistent Open World mode for up to eight co-op players, yet the core experience is still defined by harsh survival chores: finding oxygen, gathering food, stockpiling supplies, and preparing for another punishing trip into the wild.

The setting is one of the game’s main selling points, though it comes wrapped in relentless hostility. Icarus is described as a planet with a temper, and the threat list is long: toxic atmosphere, savage wildlife, apex predators, destructive storms, and forest fires. The game clearly expects careful preparation, but that also means a lot of downtime spent planning around dangers that never seem far away. Even the basic act of exploring can feel more like defensive management than discovery.

The world itself is large on paper, with over 64 square kilometers of hand-crafted terrain, hidden caves, and multiple biomes. Every tree can be harvested, every rock can be voxel mined, and every creature can be hunted. That level of interactivity sounds impressive, but it also reinforces the grind-heavy structure. Players can tame and ride mounts, build with more than eight materials, and craft advanced tech to extract resources faster, yet all of it serves the same loop of gathering, surviving, and bracing for harsher challenges.

Progression is split across three tech systems: planetary tech, player talents, and the orbital workshop. There are four tiers of technology, ranging from hand-crafted basics to advanced composites and electronics, plus over 100 talents for specializing in areas like exploring, building, farming, hunting, taming, and fighting. On paper, this breadth suggests flexibility, but it also makes the game’s structure feel overextended, as if it is trying to compensate for a thin core survival loop with layers of progression systems.

Icarus offers three main modes: Open World, timed Missions, and creative Outposts. Open World is the standard survival format, where players try to hold their ground against nature and predators. Missions are limited-time runs that send prospectors down with nothing, tasking them with mining exotics and earning Ren for powerful factions. Outposts are the more relaxed option, with regenerating resources and safer environments for creative building. Even here, though, the game seems more focused on rules and constraints than freedom, especially in the timed mission structure that forces players to work against the clock.

The lore frames the entire experience as a failed colonization effort turned resource grab. Icarus was meant to be humanity’s second Earth, but terraforming collapsed, the atmosphere became toxic, and the planet became a symbol of failure. When exotic matter was discovered as the cause and as a valuable resource, interest returned in the form of contracts, extraction, and political tension. Prospectors arrive, complete jobs, convert exotics into advanced tech, and return for more dangerous drops. It is a bleak premise, and the game leans heavily into that bleakness, though not always in a way that feels compelling.

In the end, **ICARUS** is built around survival pressure, co-op cooperation, and a dense crafting-and-progression framework, but it also sounds exhausting by design. Its large handcrafted world, layered tech trees, and multiple play modes give it structure, yet the overall impression is one of relentless punishment disguised as adventure. For players who enjoy being worn down by systems, it may hold some appeal. For everyone else, it looks like a hard-fought expedition into a planet that cannot wait to get rid of you.
