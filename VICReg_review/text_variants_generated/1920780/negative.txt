*Stray Gods: The Roleplaying Musical* is a modern fantasy game that tries to dress up a familiar interactive fiction setup as something more ambitious than it really is. You play as Grace, a college dropout who is suddenly given the power of a Muse and then pushed into the usual race-against-time scenario: uncover the truth behind her predecessor’s death before time runs out. The premise promises urgency and mythic intrigue, but it mostly leans on established fantasy scaffolding and a name-dropping approach to Greek mythology.

The game is built around choice, but the store page makes clear that its core appeal is simply branching narrative management. You decide who Grace allies with, who she trusts, and who might betray her, with outcomes that change both the ending and the route taken to get there. That sounds reactive, yet the description is careful to emphasize “thousands of potential variations” without showing anything especially substantial beyond conversation-driven consequences. As with many choice-based games, the promise of individuality may end up feeling more decorative than transformative.

Its biggest gimmick is the “roleplaying musical” angle, and this is where the game seems most self-conscious. You are told to charm, negotiate, or strong-arm your way through the story via fully interactive musical numbers composed by a list of industry names. The pitch suggests that every scene is layered with orchestration, performance, and player input, but it also sounds like a heavily curated spectacle rather than a truly freeform system. The game appears more interested in presenting itself as a crossover event than in offering mechanical depth.

The setting is a hidden world of mythic Greek gods and monsters, but the store page suggests a revisionist take that relies on contrast rather than reinvention. These are “familiar names” with “not so familiar stories,” which is another way of saying the game expects recognition to do much of the work. You meet a cast of “hilarious, enigmatic, and compelling characters,” yet the page gives little reason to believe this won’t collapse into the standard parade of quirky mythological personalities and melodramatic interpersonal tension.

Relationship-building seems to be a central pillar, though again the description makes it sound broader than it may actually be. Grace can build friendships with mortals and gods, and romance is offered through four potential paramours. That gives the game a dating-sim flavor, but the framing is still tied tightly to the same choice menu structure. Rather than offering meaningful roleplaying freedom, it looks like the player is being guided through predetermined emotional beats wrapped in songs.

Presentation is clearly one of the main selling points. The game is described as beautifully hand-illustrated, fully orchestrated, and performed by an all-star cast, with voice actors pulled from video games, television, and Broadway. That is an impressive production pitch, but it also feels like a lot of effort has been put into selling prestige and atmosphere in place of complexity. The page repeatedly insists that character and story take center stage, which can be read as a polite warning that traditional gameplay systems are minimal.

In the end, *Stray Gods* seems to be more of a stylized narrative experiment than a robust roleplaying game. It offers music, mythology, romance, and branching dialogue, but everything is filtered through a heavily authored experience that leaves little room for true player agency. For those who want a theatrical story with songs, it may be passable. For anyone expecting a substantial RPG, it looks more like a polished, self-important curiosity than a genuinely flexible adventure.
