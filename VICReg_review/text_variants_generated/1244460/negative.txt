*Jurassic World Evolution 2* is a disappointing management sim that tries to build on its 2018 predecessor but mainly offers more of the same with a few added layers. It presents itself as a sequel with an all-new narrative campaign voiced by cast members from the Jurassic World film franchise, along with four game modes and a larger roster of prehistoric species. Yet despite the bigger scope, the game still sounds like a checklist of features rather than a truly fresh evolution.

The Campaign mode is centered on the aftermath of *Jurassic World: Fallen Kingdom*, placing players in the role of a park management operator trying to control, conserve, and contain wild dinosaurs across the USA. Familiar characters such as Dr. Ian Malcolm and Claire Dearing return, but the promise of a dramatic original story does not change the fact that the core experience remains management-heavy and formulaic. Players are expected to juggle customisable buildings, hire Scientists, and shape parks around the needs of guests and dinosaurs, which may appeal to genre fans but also suggests a cluttered, overly administrative structure.

Beyond the campaign, Sandbox mode is there for unrestricted experimentation, while Challenge mode pushes players into dealing with different locations and environmental calamities. The game also includes “what-if” scenarios inspired by Jurassic World and Jurassic Park films, with levels set across eras and locations from all five movies. That sounds ambitious, but it is still fundamentally built around replaying film moments with minor twists rather than offering anything truly original.

The creature roster is the main attraction, with over 75 prehistoric species, including flying and marine reptiles. Deluxe Edition adds five more animals: Geosternbergia, Attenborosaurus, Pachyrhinosaurus, Huayangosaurus, and Megalosaurus, plus six building signs and three vehicle skins for the Ranger Team, Capture Team, and Mobile Vet Unit in Challenge and Sandbox modes. The dinosaurs are said to feel more alive than before, with territory behavior, dominance fights, and environmental reactions, and bioengineering lets players alter colors and genomes. Still, these systems sound like incremental feature additions rather than meaningful innovation.

In the end, *Jurassic World Evolution 2* looks like a dense but overly familiar management game that leans hard on the franchise’s branding. It promises more dinosaurs, more modes, and more customization, but not necessarily a better or more exciting experience.
