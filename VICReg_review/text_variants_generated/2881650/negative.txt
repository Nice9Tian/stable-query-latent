Content Warning is a co-op horror game that leans heavily on the gimmick of becoming internet-famous by filming danger instead of avoiding it, and that premise does most of the work. You and up to three other players descend into the so-called Old World with a handheld camera, a diving bell, and not much else worth praising. The goal is simple and bleak: record spooky incidents, survive long enough to get the footage back, and dump it onto SpöökTube in the hope that the algorithm rewards your bad decisions.

The game’s structure is built around a three-day cycle, where each run is split into a short pursuit of views and ad revenue. The more horrifying material you capture, the more “viral” your team becomes, assuming you don’t collapse first from oxygen loss, dead batteries, or the usual cooperative disaster of losing friends along the way. If anyone survives the descent, the group has to retreat to the diving bell and make it back to the surface before the entire outing turns into a wasted stunt. That loop sounds straightforward, but it also feels like the game is aware that repetition is part of the joke, since your progress is mostly measured in clicks, views, and the kind of clout that seems barely worth the risk.

What the Old World actually contains is a collection of physics-animated monsters, cursed relics, and other artefacts, all framed as material for content rather than genuine exploration. The game encourages players to keep filming as many scary things as possible, which makes the horror feel more like forced spectacle than meaningful tension. There are flashlights, gear upgrades, emotes, props, and medical equipment, but the whole setup still seems designed around scrambling for survival with tools that only slightly delay failure. Even the customization is deliberately silly, with an ASCII face customiser and the ability to make and save in-game recordings, as if the game wants to dress up the chaos without actually deepening it.

Content Warning supports 2-4 player online co-op and voice chat, which is unsurprising given that the entire experience depends on friends reacting badly in real time. The store page also emphasizes sponsorship deals, island upgrades, and hats, further reducing the whole thing to a shallow economy of novelty purchases and internet branding. Ad revenue from uploaded videos is the main progression system, and every success pushes players toward better gear so they can produce slightly less doomed footage next time. It is an unflattering loop of exploitation, vanity, and noisy survival, and the game seems content to remain trapped inside that narrow joke.
