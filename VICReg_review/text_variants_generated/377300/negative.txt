Thunder Tier One presents itself as a realistic top-down shooter, but its ambitions are weighed down by a narrow, grimly familiar setup and a heavy emphasis on procedure over excitement. Set in the 1990s, sometime after the fall of the USSR, the game casts players as part of an elite special operations unit sent to stop a terrorist organization called SBR in the Eastern European nation of Salobia. The premise leans on the usual counterterrorism framework, with the campaign focused on tracking down SBR leader Marko Antonov, yet the story setup does little more than provide a backdrop for mission-based tactics.

The game is built around a 9-mission, story-driven campaign designed primarily for 4-player co-op, though solo play is supported with three AI teammates. In theory, the team-based structure should create tension and coordination, but the actual design appears to hinge on constant micromanagement and communication rather than momentum or variety. The AI teammates can be given individual and group commands, and players are told they can control how much they manage them, but this sounds more like a burden than a strength. Even the solo mode is framed as an extension of the same tactical routine, with intelligent AI behavior serving mainly to keep the system functioning.

Customization is clearly one of the game’s main pillars, but again it feels more utilitarian than compelling. Players choose every element of their loadout before each mission: weapons, attachments, ammo type and quantity, armor, grenades, tools, and even camouflage patterns. The same control applies to AI teammates. While this level of preparation may appeal to players who enjoy fiddling with gear, it also suggests a game that spends too much time in menus and planning screens. The choice between a stealth approach with suppressors, IR lasers, and NVGs, or a louder assault build with heavy armor and extra ammo, is straightforward enough, but the game seems to reduce tactical identity to loadout selection.

Thunder Tier One also stresses its “realistic gunplay,” and this is where it tries to separate itself from ordinary top-down shooters. Weapons have detailed attributes including handling, rate of fire, muzzle velocity, effective range, and accuracy. Ammunition type affects precision, damage against armored and unarmored targets, penetration through cover, and even the damage and trajectory of rounds after passing through objects. On paper, this level of simulation adds depth, but it also sounds punishing and cumbersome, especially for a game built from a top-down perspective. Rather than encouraging fluid action, it seems designed to make every shot and every encounter feel bureaucratic and exacting.

Beyond the campaign, the game includes several multiplayer modes. Exfil is a 4v4 mode centered on escorting a package out within a time limit while the other team tries to stop it. Advance and Secure pits two 4v4 teams against each other to capture sectors. Death Match is a free-for-all mode for up to eight players. Domination is a PvE mode for up to four players, where the team races to secure checkpoints, disarm explosives, and survive counter-attacks. These modes offer some variety, but they still revolve around the same restrained tactical framework rather than delivering anything especially distinctive.

There is also full modding support planned, with Steam Workshop integration allowing players to modify missions, weapons, AI behavior, equipment, and more. That may extend the game’s lifespan, but it also feels like an admission that the core package may not be enough on its own. Thunder Tier One seems obsessed with realism, coordination, and loadout management, yet the result is a shooter that appears more severe than thrilling, more methodical than engaging, and too eager to mistake technical detail for genuine tension.
