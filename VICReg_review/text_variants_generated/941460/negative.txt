*Death Trash* presents itself as a grim blend of post-apocalyptic, horror, and cyberpunk ideas, but the result sounds more like a pile of familiar genre ingredients than anything genuinely striking. The game centers on a world where “cosmic horrors long for humanity,” yet humanity is reduced to punks with shotguns, outcasts, and worm-related oddities. It aims for a fresh personality, but the premise mostly leans on shock imagery and genre shorthand.

At its core, *Death Trash* is an open-world, single-player role-playing game with handcrafted levels, a strong story campaign, and optional local co-op. It tries to fuse old-school RPG depth with modern controls and feel, which is an ambitious promise, though the feature list suggests a fairly standard mix of systems rather than anything especially inventive. Combat is real-time and includes ranged and melee attacks, stealth, dialogue choices, inventory management, crafting, and skills such as pickpocketing and puking. That last detail may be memorable, but it also hints at a game that values gross-out novelty over substance.

The world design seems deliberately gritty and unpleasant: a post-apocalyptic wasteland crossed by world map travel and dotted with hand-painted organic locations. Players are told they can meet characters, talk to the Fleshkraken, visit the Puke Bar, and enter old temples in search of “expanding your awareness,” a phrase that sounds more like awkward style than meaningful depth. Character customization exists through stats and in-game decisions, and the game promises story progression, side quests, and freedom to fight machines and larger-than-life monsters or even befriend them.

That freedom, however, is framed in a way that feels more like a checklist than a compelling design philosophy. The game advertises manual saving, the ability to leave dialogue anytime, and the option to kill everyone or kill no one, as if openness alone were enough to carry the experience. It comes across as trying very hard to reassure players that they can do anything, while offering little evidence that those choices will matter in a particularly interesting way.

The story follows humanity after its expansion into the stars and settlement on the planet Nexus, where ancient secrets of stone and flesh were uncovered and civilization collapsed into enclosed habitats. The wasteland is now ruled by outcasts, machine controllers, and offspring of the worm. You play as one of those outcasts, burdened with something dangerous, and sent through deserts, ruins, underground spaces, and beyond. It is the kind of bleak setup that feels deliberately oppressive, though not especially original.

The local co-op mode is the one area where the game at least sounds somewhat thoughtful. It covers the full campaign, lets a second player drop in and out at any time, gives them their own character and inventory, and keeps quest progress shared. Both players stay in the same location, travel together on the world map, and can act separately without interrupting one another. There is shared-screen play by default and an optional permanent split-screen mode. Still, online co-op is not planned due to limited resources, which makes the feature feel constrained rather than expansive.

Overall, *Death Trash* appears to be a dense collection of RPG mechanics, dark worldbuilding, and crude thematic flourishes that aim for attitude more than elegance. It offers a lot on paper, but much of it seems bound together by gloomy aesthetics and a desire to sound edgy, rather than by a clear sense of polish or distinct identity.
