Battlefield 1 is presented as a grand return to the devastation of World War I, but beneath the marketing it remains the same familiar shooter formula stretched across bigger explosions and louder chaos. It asks players to “join the strong Battlefield community” and plunge into “epic battles,” yet the promise is less about depth than spectacle. The base game offers an “adventure-filled campaign” and large-scale multiplayer battles with up to 64 players, while the Revolution edition simply bundles in the Premium Pass and several themed packs.

The game’s selling points are bluntly conventional: fight as infantry, or take control of land, air, and sea vehicles. Maps span besieged French cities, the Italian Alps, and Arabian deserts, with fully destructible environments and shifting weather meant to keep battles unpredictable. In practice, this just means more ways for the battlefield to collapse around you. The game also leans heavily on oversized machines such as tanks, biplanes, Behemoths, airships, armored trains, and dreadnoughts, suggesting that raw scale is meant to compensate for the chaos of its design.

Multiplayer is clearly the center of the package. Up to 64 players are thrown into team-based fights, cavalry charges, and the new Operations mode, where attackers push across linked maps while defenders try to hold the line. The structure sounds ambitious, but it is still built around familiar attrition and escalation rather than anything meaningfully new. The additional expansion packs and themed hero bundles add content, but mostly as padding around the same core experience.

Battlefield 1 wants to feel like a sweeping war epic, yet it ultimately reduces one of history’s most catastrophic conflicts into destructible scenery, vehicle showcases, and coordinated firefights. It is loud, crowded, and deliberately cinematic, but it also feels trapped by its own obsession with scale.
