Ghostrunner 2 tries to sell itself as a hardcore first-person slasher, but beneath the flashy cyberpunk presentation it mostly feels like a familiar formula stretched across more systems and cosmetics. Set one year after the original game, it follows Jack as he returns to a post-apocalyptic world after the fall of the Keymaster, facing a violent AI cult outside Dharma Tower. The premise sounds dramatic, yet the page leans heavily on style over substance, dressing up a straightforward revenge mission in enough neon and synthwave to distract from how derivative it all is.

The core appeal is still katana combat, and the store page proudly promises “incredible” mechanics, but the description also makes clear that Ghostrunner 2 is trying to soften its own harsh edges with “greater accessibility” and new skills. That sounds less like a bold reinvention and more like an attempt to make a punishing game slightly less punishing. Enemies reportedly react differently depending on which skills are used against them, and the progression system has been completely redone so players can experiment and customize. In practice, this reads like a set of adjustments meant to pad out replayability rather than a truly fresh design.

The game also adds nonlinear levels and motorbike sections, which are presented as major highlights. Unfortunately, the store text suggests these sections are more about spectacle than depth, inserted to vary the pace rather than deepen the experience. Boss fights are now more interactive, with players given freedom to choose how to survive, but that framing implies a combat system that needs extra choice just to keep battles from feeling too rigid.

Ghostrunner 2 also pushes its “immersive, mind-bending” features, including exploding barrels, destructible walls, and neutral entities, as if basic environmental interaction were a major innovation. A new dialogue system is included for players who want to dive deeper into the lore and plot, though the need to emphasize that says a lot about how thin the narrative may be without it. The whole package feels assembled to sound ambitious, but it often comes across as a checklist of modern action-game features rather than a coherent leap forward.

Even the sound design is used as a selling point, with a synthwave soundtrack featuring Daniel Deluxe, We Are Magonia, Gost, Dan Terminus, and Arek Reikowski. It is hard to escape the impression that Ghostrunner 2 is relying on atmosphere to carry a game that is otherwise still built around the same brutal cyber-ninja fantasy. The various editions add little beyond cosmetics such as swords, hand skins, a motorcycle skin, and a season pass, which only reinforces how much of the experience is tied to surface-level style.

Ghostrunner 2 is a slick, aggressive sequel that seems determined to keep slicing through enemies, but the store page makes it sound more like an expansion of the same idea than a meaningful evolution. It offers speed, violence, and cyberpunk flair, yet the description itself leaves the feeling that the game is doing a lot of posturing to conceal how standard it really is.
