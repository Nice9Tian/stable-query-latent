Techtonica presents itself as a factory automation game, but it leans heavily on atmosphere and mystery to distract from how familiar its core systems are. Set beneath the surface of the alien world of Calyx, Groundbreaker, it asks players to start with the bare minimum for digging, mining, and production, then slowly expand into larger operations. That progression sounds promising, but the page makes it clear that the game is built around the usual automation loop: gather resources, build machines, connect production lines, and keep scaling upward.

The campaign is one of its main selling points, but even that is framed as a story-driven wrapper for standard genre mechanics. Techtonica offers a fully voice-acted campaign, which is unusual for a factory automation game, yet the premise is fairly routine: an expedition has gone wrong, something unknown waits below, and you are left to untangle the mess with the help of cubic companions who supposedly have their own motives and secrets. It may sound ambitious, but the page gives little reason to expect anything beyond a conventional sci-fi mystery structure.

Gameplay appears centered on expanding machinery and carving out space, with the game constantly encouraging players to dig deeper and produce more efficiently. Specialized machines, power systems, conveyors, and inserters are all part of the setup, and the world itself is treated as something to be dismantled and reshaped. The MOLE, described as a black hole gun, lets you destroy stone walls, while towering pumps can drain deserts of sand. This kind of terraforming is probably the most distinctive idea here, though it mostly serves the practical goal of making room for more industrial sprawl.

Exploration is presented as meaningful, but again it seems tied to the standard rewards of the genre. Players tunnel toward ore deposits, research facilities, hidden caves, ancient artifacts, and new technologies. The page tries to sell this as discovery, but it is still just a loop of chasing resources and unlocking upgrades. Even the visual appeal of the bioluminescent alien environment feels like decoration layered over a game whose main objective is optimization.

Techtonica also supports solo play and up to four-player co-op, which broadens its appeal, though not necessarily its originality. Its feature list emphasizes factory automation, travel systems, first-person exploration, and an original soundtrack by Cityfires, all of which sound competent rather than especially daring. Overall, Techtonica seems to be another automation game that hopes its subterranean sci-fi setting, voice acting, and terraforming tools will make the genre feel fresher than it really is.
