American Truck Simulator is presented as a large-scale trucking simulator, but beneath the polish it is mostly a slow, repetitive drive through a few western U.S. states. The game sends players across California, Nevada, and Arizona, promising iconic landmarks and scenic routes, yet the experience is still fundamentally about hauling cargo from one point to another. Its structure borrows heavily from Euro Truck Simulator 2, which means the formula is already familiar, and simply expanding it with more features does not make it any less methodical or grind-heavy.

You begin as a hired driver in the local freight market and are expected to climb toward ownership and company management. That progression sounds ambitious, but it is really just a long chain of deliveries, skill improvements, and business expansion. The game allows you to buy trucks, build a fleet, hire drivers, and manage garages, but all of this sits on top of the same core loop of safe delivery and route planning. It is a business simulation wrapped around an even slower driving simulation, which may appeal to some, but for many players it will feel more like work than entertainment.

The trucks themselves are detailed and officially licensed, and the customization system gives you options such as cabins, chassis, paint jobs, tuning accessories, and stronger engines. You can also change your cab setup and adjust your position for a better view, which adds some immersion, though not much excitement. Cargo variety is decent, ranging from food and machinery to hazardous materials, and the trailer selection includes reefers, flatbeds, dumpers, lowboys, and goosenecks. Still, even with longer trailers up to 53 feet, the challenge is mostly about patience, parking, and avoiding mistakes rather than offering real variety in gameplay.

American Truck Simulator also leans heavily into simulation details like air brakes, retarder, Jake brake, trailer brake, transmission types based on real trucks, and brake intensity settings. These features may impress trucking enthusiasts, but for everyone else they only reinforce how mechanically dense and slow the game can feel. The police will fine you for breaking speed limits, weigh stations can check your load, and you are encouraged to follow road rules carefully, which makes every trip feel cautious and restrictive instead of liberating. The route adviser is there as a helper, but it also underlines how much the game depends on guided routine rather than spontaneous play.

The game’s broader presentation is functional rather than thrilling. There is support for steering wheels, gamepads, and other input devices, plus a built-in music player, radio streaming, and a photo mode with editing options. Steam achievements and trading cards are included as expected, and long-term support is mentioned as a selling point. World of Trucks adds an optional online layer for sharing screenshots, using custom avatars and license plates, and browsing other players’ images, but this is mostly supplementary content rather than something that changes the core experience.

Ultimately, American Truck Simulator is a precise and feature-rich trucking sim that knows exactly what it is, but that also means it offers very little outside its narrow niche. It is slow, repetitive, and highly structured, with much of its appeal resting on patience, realism, and a willingness to treat long hauls as entertainment. For players who want a detailed virtual truck job, it may suffice; for everyone else, it is likely to feel like an exhausting drive with too little payoff.
