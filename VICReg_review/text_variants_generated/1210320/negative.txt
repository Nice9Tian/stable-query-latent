Potion Craft: Alchemist Simulator presents itself as a charming alchemy sandbox, but beneath the peculiar presentation it is mostly a slow, fiddly routine of grinding ingredients, stirring cauldrons, and dragging potions through an Alchemy Map. The game leans heavily on its medieval-manuscript-inspired visuals and “physical” interactions, but the actual appeal is limited to people who enjoy repetitive crafting systems and micromanagement disguised as whimsy.

You are given leaves, flowers, berries, roots, fruits, minerals, and mushrooms to work with, then expected to grind them finely, heat the coals, boil, stir, and choose a base such as water or oil. The game frames this as learning the “art of potion making,” but it sounds more impressive than it is. Progress depends on experimenting with routes on the Alchemy Map to combine effects, while the game also reminds you that common sense matters unless you want to do absurd things like selling a poisonous healing potion. That kind of freedom is marketed as clever roleplay, yet it is mostly just an excuse for chaotic trial and error.

The selling loop is equally blunt. Townsfolk arrive each day with problems, and you decide what to hand them, knowing there will be consequences. You can attract guilds, befriend or feud with notable figures, gain riches and influence, and potentially affect the fate of the town. In theory, this sounds like a living economy with moral weight, but in practice it is still a shop-management structure wrapped in flavor text about being a noble artisan, a greedy weasel, or some occult dark master. The game invites roleplaying, but it does not sound especially deep about it.

Potion Craft: Alchemist Simulator also asks you to source ingredients by buying from traveling merchants or growing your own. Buying can be expensive, though haggling may reduce the cost. On top of that, there is customization for bottles, labels, icons, colors, names, and descriptions, along with the ability to arrange your creations on shelves and tables. These are the kinds of decorative touches that might seem delightful at first, but they cannot fully mask how much of the experience is still centered on repetitive preparation, commerce, and tinkering.

Overall, the game appears built around a neat visual style and a broad list of systems, but those systems mostly translate into busywork, cautious experimentation, and selling potions to demanding customers. It may satisfy players who want a sandbox of alchemy-themed chores, but it does not sound like it offers much beyond that.
