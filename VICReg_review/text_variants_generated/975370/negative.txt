Dwarf Fortress is less a game than an exhausting monument to overcomplication. It presents itself as the “deepest, most intricate simulation” ever made, and that claim is both its selling point and its burden. The world is not merely generated in a superficial sense; civilizations rise and fall, personalities and cultures are simulated, creatures populate the map, and the whole thing is designed to feel endless. If that sounds intimidating, it should. The game seems determined to turn even basic play into a long-term management headache.

At its core, you build a fortress and try to keep a colony of dwarves alive. In practice, that means managing food, beer, wealth, construction, and a constantly expanding list of dangers. Starvation, dragons, madness, hostile civilizations, the wilderness, the dead, aquifers, and simple bad planning all threaten to ruin everything. The page openly admits that your dwarves will probably mine themselves toward eventual demise, which is a pretty accurate summary of the experience: you are not so much building a thriving settlement as watching a disaster slowly assemble itself.

The simulation’s scope is almost absurd. The combat system tracks skills, body parts, material properties, aimed attacks, wrestling, pain, nausea, poison effects, and more. The world generation also includes hundreds of animals and monsters, many of them randomized for each world, plus generated poetry, musical forms, instruments, and dances for dwarves to practice. Even the weather is modeled with wind, humidity, air masses, fronts, clouds, storms, and blizzards. Over two hundred rock and mineral types appear in their proper geological environments. It is an impressive pile of systems, but it also feels like a game that delights in burying the player under detail whether they asked for it or not.

The Steam version adds graphics support and music by default, but the page is careful to insist that nothing substantial has changed under the hood. In other words, this is still the same old Dwarf Fortress, only now with pixel art and a soundtrack. The game also leans on a new-player tutorial system, which is probably necessary, since the original experience was notoriously hostile to newcomers. Even with guidance, it still sounds like the kind of game that makes learning feel like a prerequisite for suffering rather than fun.

There are some interesting ambitions buried in the chaos. Dwarves have their own simulated personalities and thoughts, and you are expected to read those thoughts if you want to keep them happy. You can build floodgates for farming or magma control, establish a barony, and support increasingly demanding nobility. Your fortress can develop its own culture through taverns, libraries, temples, honey, wax, pottery, animal training, and bookbinding. But these features are framed less as rewards and more as additional obligations layered onto an already overloaded simulation.

The presentation is also heavily sold as a legacy project: updated since 2003, created by the Adams brothers, and still “only about 44% done” by Bay 12’s own estimate. That unfinished quality is part of the identity, but it also reinforces how much of the experience is built around perpetual incompleteness. Even the famous motto “Losing is fun!” feels less like irony and more like a warning.

Dwarf Fortress is historically important, influential, and undeniably ambitious. It inspired games like RimWorld, Prison Architect, and Minecraft, and it was even acquired by the Museum of Modern Art in New York. But as a playable experience, it remains an intimidating, sprawling, and often punishing simulation that seems more interested in proving its depth than in making that depth pleasant to navigate.
