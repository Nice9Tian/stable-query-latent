Songs of Syx presents itself as an ambitious fantasy city-builder, but the store page makes it painfully clear that ambition is doing most of the heavy lifting. The game is in Early Access, has spent years as a prototype, and the page repeatedly warns that the current version is still unpolished, incomplete, and not ready to deliver everything it promises. That warning is not subtle, and for good reason.

The core premise is straightforward enough: you begin with a tiny colony and attempt to grow it into a sprawling metropolis and eventually an empire. In practice, the page sells this as a blend of city-building, grand strategy, and large-scale tactical warfare, all wrapped in a low-fantasy setting. You are meant to manage production chains, keep people alive through winter, expand into huge settlements, and maintain a kingdom made up of villages, ports, castles, and lumber camps. It also insists that small mistakes can snowball into collapse, which sounds less like elegant strategy and more like a constant threat of systems buckling under their own complexity.

The world is described as “living,” with weather, day and night cycles, migrations, droughts, pestilence, pirates, bandits, and random discoveries shaping every playthrough. All content, background stories, and easter eggs are said to be generated uniquely each time, though the game still hangs everything on a fixed backstory about gods, chaos, war, and the broken world of Syx. That lore sounds grand, but the page never hides the fact that the game is still far from a finished state where such worldbuilding would feel fully integrated.

At the settlement level, Songs of Syx emphasizes large-scale population management. Your city can grow to tens of thousands of inhabitants, and you’re expected to build enough infrastructure to support them while avoiding overextension. Everything requires upkeep, and even a small disruption in production or public standing can trigger collapse. People are not handled as abstract numbers for long: each subject is supposedly an individual with a personality, agenda, preferences, friends, and traits. You do not directly control them, only issue plans and hope they behave intelligently. If they are treated well, more may immigrate; if mistreated, they may work inefficiently or revolt. The game clearly wants this to feel deep, but the page’s repeated emphasis on unrest, rebellion, and instability makes the whole experience sound more exhausting than rewarding.

Society is also built around groups defined by race, social standing, religion, and trade, and the game openly says subjects dislike those who are different from them. You are allowed to encourage discrimination and privileges, or try to build a tolerant society instead. That may be a meaningful systems choice, but the store text frames it with a harsh, almost cynical edge that fits the game’s overall tone: manage people, exploit them, or face the consequences.

On the strategic layer, the page describes a kingdom that must be sustained by a wider network of settlements and relationships. Other kingdoms and despots will form alliances or expand aggressively, so diplomacy matters, though the advice is mostly to avoid drawing cultural attention. Trade is based on supply and demand, with no fixed prices and no currency to mint, which means wealth can fluctuate wildly. Again, the design sounds interesting in theory, but the description keeps returning to volatility and fragility, as if the game is determined to punish curiosity with complexity.

Combat is one of the page’s biggest selling points, but also one of its most suspect. Songs of Syx promises massive battles with up to 20,000 individual units and clashes involving up to 40,000. You can gather troops from allies and vassals, recruit mercenaries and heroes, and try to use tactics, encirclement, and morale pressure to win. Battles are described as realistic, with low kill rates and fragile morale, and the outcome is supposed to have serious consequences. If you lose, enemies may hunt fleeing troops and annihilate your army, forcing you into costly peace. That may sound dramatic, but it also suggests a game where one bad engagement can undo a huge amount of slow progress.

The game also includes systems for nobility and slaves. Nobles can be appointed or imported from other kingdoms to manage parts of your realm and improve production and standing, but the page explicitly warns that they may betray you or side with enemies. Slaves are described in a crude and openly exploitative way, with the game framing them as a labor force that must be kept from revolting. The page presents these mechanics without much nuance, which only makes the whole setting feel more bleak and unpleasant.

Races are another major focus. The player can choose the Dondorians, who are portrayed as masterful miners obsessed with Dyhrlium and rebuilding their legendary mountain capital, or Men, who are fragile but clever and politically ambitious. There are also the Kantors, reclusive and wrathful survivors from the frozen north, who may be rare but valuable if they can be attracted to your city. The setting’s broad fantasy background involves gods of order and chaos, an ancient collapse, plague, undead armies, magical destruction, and an icy age that has only recently receded. It is the sort of lore that tries very hard to feel epic, yet the page admits the game is still working toward the basic foundation of its systems.

The soundtrack is one of the few things presented without caveats, though even that is tied to Kickstarter stretch goals and a playable prototype. The page includes enthusiastic quotes from prototype players, but these testimonials mostly reinforce the impression that the current build is promising rather than complete. The developers themselves say the base game only contains about 60–70% of the features, many of which are still unpolished. They also admit the release will only include a portion of the listed content, with some features possibly cut and others added later. In other words, this is not a finished strategy epic but a long-term project still trying to become one.

Songs of Syx seems determined to be a city builder, grand strategy game, population simulator, and large-scale war game all at once. Whether it will ever fully achieve that is another matter. Right now, based on its own store page, it reads less like a polished game and more like an immense, complicated prototype asking players to buy in early and tolerate the mess while it slowly becomes what it wants to be.
