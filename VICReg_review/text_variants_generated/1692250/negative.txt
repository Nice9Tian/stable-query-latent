F1® 22 is presented as the official video game of the 2022 FIA Formula One World Championship™, but it does little to hide how heavily it leans on superficial upgrades and paid extras. The game claims to usher players into “the new era of Formula 1®,” yet much of the pitch is built around cosmetic content, premium editions, and virtual currency rather than meaningful innovation.

The game’s core selling points are the redesigned cars, overhauled rules, and the addition of the Miami International Autodrome. It also includes Formula 1® and Formula 2™ 2022 season cars with the full lineup of drivers, teams, and liveries, which sounds authentic enough on paper. But the overall impression is one of repackaging an annual sports title with a few new surfaces layered on top. Even the promise of more “immersive or broadcast race sequences” feels like a presentation tweak rather than a real transformation.

Career and multiplayer options are broad, but not especially exciting. My Team returns with “new depth,” while players can also race in split-screen, Two-Player Career, and multiplayer. There is also the all-new Pirelli Hot Laps feature, which lets players take supercars from famous names in the sport out onto the track. Again, this sounds more like a side distraction than a substantial addition to the Formula 1 experience.

The game also pushes F1® Life, a lifestyle-focused space where players can decorate with furniture, wall art, and clothing items. Rather than deepening the racing, this feature feels like an awkward attempt to inject glamour and personalization into a game that should be focused on competition. It comes across as a thin layer of style pasted over a familiar formula.

The Champions Edition further highlights the monetized, bonus-driven structure of the package. It includes three new My Team Icons — Nico Hülkenberg, Mark Webber, and Jacques Villeneuve — along with two drivable safety cars, but only in Time Trial Mode: the Mercedes-AMG GT Black Series Safety Car and the Aston Martin Vantage Safety Car. It also throws in 18,000 PitCoin, an F1® Life Starter Pack, and New Era content such as a livery, suit, helmet, cap, and T-shirt. Rather than making the game feel richer, these extras mainly reinforce how aggressively it is designed around add-ons and incentives.

Visually, the game advertises raytraced shadows and reflections, though that requires raytracing graphics hardware. That technical note matters because it suggests the game is eager to market visual polish, even as its underlying structure remains tied to the standard annual sports-game routine. It also includes optional in-game purchases of virtual currency for virtual items, which is exactly the kind of monetization that makes the whole package feel less like a complete racing game and more like a storefront wrapped in Formula 1 branding.

In the end, F1® 22 looks less like a bold leap forward and more like a cautious, heavily marketed update. It offers official licensing, current-season content, and a handful of new features, but the emphasis on cosmetics, premium bonuses, and in-game purchases makes it hard to see as anything more than another polished but uninspired entry in the series.
