Xenonauts 2 is a grim, punishing strategy game that asks you to run a secret multinational war against an alien invasion and makes the whole effort feel exhausting by design. Set in 2009, with the Cold War still dragging on and the Berlin Wall still standing, it imagines a world where UFO sightings, disappearances, and panic are becoming commonplace while a covert organization tries to respond from the shadows. The premise is clear, but the game seems determined to bury it under layers of management, tradeoffs, and attrition.

At its core, the game splits your attention between a global strategy layer and dangerous tactical battles on both land and in the air. You are expected to build and manage multiple bases around the world, and those bases are not simple menu hubs but carefully planned facilities with radar stations, hangars, research teams, operatives, manufacturing, defenses, and transport capacity. Placement matters because fuel ranges and geographic coverage shape how effectively you can respond to threats. The grid-based construction system and the need to specialize bases for detection, rapid response, research, or manufacturing add logistical pressure, but not in a particularly welcoming way. Resources, money, and time are all permanently scarce, so nearly every decision feels like a compromise rather than a satisfying plan.

Combat offers little relief. Ground missions demand constant attention to positioning, ammunition, lines of sight, cover, and terrain, with aliens actively hunting you instead of waiting politely to be discovered. They move intelligently, seek out your troops, and use high ground and cover against you, which means every operation is an exercise in caution and risk management. You can strike quickly with explosives, grenades, and missiles, or take a slower approach to secure alien bodies and technology for autopsies and reverse-engineering, but either path brings its own costs. Choosing to use vehicles and mechanized combat platforms can turn a battle in your favor, yet that also reduces your ability to collect intact alien materials, reinforcing the game’s obsession with unpleasant tradeoffs.

The air war is handled as real-time tactical combat, where you command fighter wings against UFOs and must juggle maneuvering, target priority, fuel, and base proximity. There are no forced one-on-one duels, so a heavily armed alien craft can be met by a coordinated squadron if you have enough infrastructure in place. That sounds flexible, but it mostly means the strategic layer becomes even more demanding, since air defense is tied directly to how well you positioned and supplied your global network of bases. When the system works, it is because you planned several steps ahead; when it does not, the game is quick to punish you.

Research and development are another major burden. The tech tree contains over one hundred projects and covers aircraft, vehicles, weapons, armor, battlefield equipment, alien-derived tech, autonomous combat platforms, and sentry guns. In theory this gives you room to tailor your approach, but in practice it mostly reinforces the feeling that you are always behind. You have to balance whether to rush automated platforms for defense, build more dropships for response capacity, or improve fighter armaments to challenge alien airpower. Training centers can improve soldiers during downtime, but the game makes it clear that actual combat experience is far more valuable, which is a rather bleak way to structure progression when so many troops are expected to be wounded or killed.

The enemy variety is broad, and the game also includes human agents called Cleaners, who work alongside the extraterrestrial threat. Their presence adds more mission types, more locations, and more objectives, but it also widens the web of enemies without making the experience feel any less oppressive. You can infiltrate their bases, secure data, and uncover more of the conspiracy, yet every engagement is framed as another dangerous opportunity to learn, lose people, or both. Medals and veteran soldiers are presented as rewards for survival, but the game repeatedly stresses that survival is not guaranteed and that losses are part of the structure, not a dramatic exception.

Xenonauts 2 is standalone and does not require knowledge of the original Xenonauts, which is probably for the best given how much patience this sequel demands on its own. It offers a dense global simulation, harsh tactical combat, and a bleak story about holding Earth together under siege. For players looking for a merciless strategy game, it may deliver exactly that. For everyone else, it sounds less like a thrilling defense of humanity and more like an extended lesson in managing shortages, casualties, and constant failure.
