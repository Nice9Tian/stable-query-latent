Project CARS 3 presents itself as a broad racing package, but the way it is framed on the store page feels more like a checklist of features than a convincing identity. It offers more than 200 elite-brand race and road cars, over 120 global tracks, and a collection of race conversion kits, with progression tied to Credits and XP as you race, buy, and own hundreds of cars. You can also upgrade vehicles with realistic performance parts, customize liveries with racing- and community-inspired elements, and personalize drivers. On paper, there is plenty to do, but the page’s emphasis on quantity does not automatically translate into a meaningful racing experience.

The game also promises flexible assistance settings for all skill levels, handling that is meant to be both convincing and fun, a 24-hour cycle, dynamic all-seasons and all-weather racing, an “unrivalled” controller experience, a visceral sense of speed, intense crash effects and car-contact, and enhanced AI. Yet these claims are delivered in such a promotional tone that they invite skepticism rather than confidence. The game clearly wants to appeal to a wide audience, but the presentation suggests it is trying very hard to sound polished without proving much.

Career and multiplayer are also pushed as major pillars. There is a new career mode where players battle their way from weekend warrior to racing legend, along with new multiplayer and community modes and daily challenges. Even so, the description reads as generic and overhyped, leaning heavily on marketing language like “deeply engaging” and “fun & exciting” without offering much substance beyond broad promises. Project CARS 3 seems to be aiming for accessibility, variety, and progression, but the store page itself leaves the impression of an overdesigned racing game that relies on buzzwords more than clarity.
