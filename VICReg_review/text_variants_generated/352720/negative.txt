*The Universim* presents itself as a god-game city builder, but the result is more like a slow, self-important management grind dressed up with cosmic ambition. You are placed above a fledgling species called “Nuggets” and tasked with dragging them from the Stone Age to the Space Age, while supposedly shaping the fate of entire planets and even a galaxy. In practice, the game leans heavily on the same familiar loop of resource management, research, and survival control, only padded with a divine veneer.

The core pitch is straightforward: play god, issue commands, and decide whether to nurture or punish your followers. The game gives you more than 15 godly powers, including tornadoes, lightning strikes, and the ability to shake the earth itself. It sounds dramatic, but this also exposes the game’s tone problem: it wants the player to feel omnipotent, yet most of the experience still revolves around micromanaging basic needs and reacting to routine problems. The result is less like commanding a civilization and more like babysitting one with occasional theatrical disasters.

The Nuggets are meant to be the heart of the game. You watch them work, fight, survive, and develop across the ages, but the description makes clear that they are governed by needs and behaviors you must constantly attend to. Their progress depends on your oversight, which means the game is built around supervision rather than true strategic freedom. Even the promise of moral choice feels blunt: you can be benevolent or malevolent, but that mostly translates into deciding how kindly or cruelly you want to smite your own subjects.

Progression is driven by more than 200 research perks and technological or societal advancements, which sounds substantial until you realize that quantity does not necessarily mean depth. The game claims you can evolve your civilization through the ages, yet the emphasis on unlocking perks suggests a long checklist of upgrades rather than a genuinely dynamic society. The same issue appears in the planetary layer. Each planet is procedurally generated and supposedly offers unique ecosystems and challenges, but the randomness mainly seems designed to keep the player busy in a different map, not to transform the underlying experience.

Environmental management is another major pillar, and it is framed as a serious consequence system. Global climates respond to your decisions and your followers’ actions, and planetary balance must be maintained if your Nuggets are to survive. This sounds important, but it also feels like the game is trying too hard to turn routine resource management into a weighty ecological lecture. The threat of “ecological disaster” is presented almost as a joke, which undercuts the supposed responsibility of being a godlike caretaker.

The game also promises a deeper cosmic mystery, with a story that allegedly challenges your perception of who and what you are. This is the kind of vague hook that often signals a thin narrative stretched over an already busy management framework. Instead of making the experience feel profound, it mostly adds another layer of uncertainty to a system already overloaded with progression, ecosystem balancing, and planetary expansion.

In the end, *The Universim* is a strategy city builder wrapped in divine language, but its ambitions outpace its clarity. It offers god powers, research trees, procedural planets, and ecological management, yet these features seem more like separate checkboxes than a cohesive vision. What should feel like commanding a civilization across the cosmos instead comes across as a muddled mix of micromanagement, arbitrary punishment, and repetitive growth systems.
