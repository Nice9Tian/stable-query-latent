Tails Noir presents itself as something profound, but beneath the noir posturing it is still a linear narrative adventure that leans heavily on mood to compensate for limited agency. You play as Howard Lotor, a raccoon private eye dragged into a personal case in a dystopian Vancouver populated by anthropomorphic animals. The game frames this as an inevitable journey of change and identity, yet it makes clear from the start that you are not special, not heroic, and not really steering events so much as being carried through them.

Its core gameplay is not built around systems or challenge, but around storytelling, emotional expression, and dialogue. The experience centers on long, elaborate conversations inspired by old school CRPGs, along with exploration of the game’s environments. Dialogue choices let you shape Howard’s identity and decide what kind of person he becomes, but the structure remains linear, so the sense of control is limited to the flavor of the protagonist rather than the outcome of the story. The game promises meaningful connections with a varied cast, but the emphasis is clearly on delivering a specific narrative rather than offering meaningful freedom.

The setting tries hard to sell itself as a bleak post-noir world: a dangerous, dreary Vancouver that is “not entirely without hope.” The districts are described as both downtrodden and flourishing, but the overall tone is one of coldness, decay, and existential fatigue. The influences are worn on its sleeve — film noir, weird sci-fi, post-Soviet aesthetics, political regimes, Lynch, Kaufman, and Sartre — and the result sounds less like a coherent identity than a stack of references meant to signal depth.

Visually, the game relies on high-resolution pixel art combined with 3D effects such as dynamic lighting, rain, volumetric fog, and neon signage. It reimagines locations like Gastown, Granville, and the Fairmont Hotel within this animal-filled version of Vancouver. The presentation aims for atmosphere above all else, which may help mask the fact that the actual gameplay is thin and mostly passive.

The soundtrack follows the same approach, with a doom jazz score by Danshin and Arooj Aftab that mixes classic jazz and electronic music. It is clearly designed to reinforce the cinematic feel, but like much of Tails Noir, it seems more interested in aesthetic severity than in offering a genuinely engaging interactive experience. In the end, it is a game about existence, loss, and transformation that appears far more committed to its own melancholy style than to providing a memorable game to actually play.
