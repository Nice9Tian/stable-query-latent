DIRT 5 tries hard to sell itself as a fresh, amplified off-road racer, but beneath the marketing language it mostly looks like another loud arcade package leaning on spectacle over substance. It offers a broad mix of vehicles — from rally icons and GT cars to trucks, buggies, muscle cars, and 900bhp sprint cars — and sends them across gravel, ice, snow, and sand on more than 70 routes in 10 global locations. The problem is that the page seems far more interested in hyping “highlight-reel moments” than in convincing players there is real depth behind the chaos.

The career mode is presented as a star-driven climb under the guidance of an icon, complete with sponsorships, unique rewards, and a rival to defeat. That sounds ambitious, but the description reads more like a checklist than a compelling structure. Even the promised “biggest ever Career” feels like empty superlative padding. The same goes for the game’s “next generation of extreme racing” claim, which is difficult to take seriously when the pitch relies so heavily on generic phrases like bold, brave, and amplified.

DIRT 5 also pushes local split-screen for up to four players and online playlists for up to 12, along with objective-based modes. On paper, that sounds decent enough for social play, but the wording suggests a game trying to compensate for limited identity with feature variety. The inclusion of a livery editor and photo mode adds customization, yet these are standard extras rather than standout attractions. Even the “deepest-ever livery editor” line feels like another overblown promise.

The store page also highlights ongoing DLC and updates, including the Wild Spirits Content Pack, new career events, sponsors, customization items, new Stampede events in Morocco, and additional real-world liveries. While that shows the game is being expanded, it also reinforces the impression of a serviceable base product being padded out after release. Overall, DIRT 5 comes across as an off-road racer that talks a big game, but its description suggests style, noise, and branding are doing most of the work.
