Once Human presents itself as a survival game set in a world ruined by the alien contamination known as Stardust, but much of what it offers is the familiar grind of scavenging, managing meters, and fighting through hostile terrain. The apocalypse has already done its damage: humans, animals, plants, soil, and even water are infected, leaving players to navigate a bleak wilderness where food can be polluted, water can be dirty, and poor choices directly reduce Sanity and, by extension, maximum HP. It is an unforgiving loop that seems designed to make basic survival feel like a chore rather than a compelling struggle.

The game’s central premise is that you are a Meta-Human capable of surviving the contamination and wielding Stardust’s power, either alone or with others. In practice, this means enduring threats from monsters, many of which were once human, while also dealing with environmental hazards and the constant pressure of survival. Version 1.3 adds the PvE scenario The Way of Winter, which introduces an even more restrictive Temperature mechanic. Players are encouraged to cooperate, build and upgrade a Thermal Tower, explore a new map, face bosses, and secure new deviations, but the pitch still revolves around making survival harder before it gets easier.

Combat and progression lean heavily on loot and customization. The game advertises around 100 gun blueprints across seven categories, along with accessories, gun perks, parts, and upgrades. That sounds expansive on paper, but it also suggests a familiar dependence on collecting and tinkering just to keep weapons viable. Fighting numerous enemies and dimension-crossing bosses is framed as a way to earn powerful items and reduce Stardust pollution, yet the loop remains straightforward: kill, loot, improve, repeat.

Base building is another major feature, though it appears more functional than inspiring. Using the Territory Core, players can construct a house and relocate it at any time. The page promotes both minimalist utility setups and larger personalized homes with patios, kitchens, and garages, but the emphasis on flexibility feels like compensation for a system that mostly serves as another survival-management layer. It is less about meaningful creativity and more about setting down a safe zone in a world that otherwise offers little comfort.

Narratively, the game asks players to uncover the truth behind Stardust and its origins, while interacting with factions that may be hostile or helpful. Human settlements can be explored for stories, or players can simply eliminate bosses and leave nothing behind. This choice-driven framing gives the impression of depth, but the overall presentation still reads like a collection of survival features tied together by a vague mystery. Once Human seems determined to offer danger, crafting, building, and exploration, yet its own description makes it sound like another grim, punishing endurance test wrapped in apocalypse aesthetics.
