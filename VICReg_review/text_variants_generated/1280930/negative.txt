Astral Ascent is presented as a 2D platformer roguelite, but its appeal seems to lean heavily on familiar genre scaffolding rather than anything truly distinctive. Set in a modern fantasy world, the game casts you as one of four heroes trying to escape The Garden, an astral prison controlled by 12 bosses known as the Zodiacs. That premise sounds dramatic enough, yet the store page mainly sells the usual roguelite promise: repeated runs, incremental power growth, and a constant push to become strong enough to survive the next attempt.

The game’s core loop revolves around building a playstyle through dozens of unique spells for each character. In practice, this means experimenting with builds, optimizing abilities, and gradually becoming stronger over time. The problem is that the description reads more like a checklist of genre features than a convincing reason the game stands apart. “Each run will test your skills and your reactivity” is the kind of broad statement that could apply to countless similar games.

The 12 Zodiacs are framed as a major selling point, spread across four worlds and each given unique attacks, combat styles, backstories, and reasons for fighting you. On paper, that sounds substantial, but the page offers little beyond the promise of variety and difficulty. It is hard not to view this as another roguelite relying on boss count and presentation to compensate for a lack of clearer identity.

The four playable characters do at least offer different combat flavors. Ayla is a fast assassin with sharp blades, Kiran relies on raw strength and shockwaves, Calie uses magical gemstones to break defenses, and Octave summons ethereal weapons for both close and long range attacks. These differences suggest some build variety, but the description still feels heavily scripted around “unique” character archetypes rather than deeper mechanical innovation.

Astral Ascent also includes a story that unfolds gradually through repeated playthroughs, with more being revealed as you get stronger. That narrative structure may encourage replayability, but it also means the game asks players to keep grinding in hopes that the plot will finally justify the repetition. Even the story is sold as something assembled “one piece at a time,” which reinforces the sense that progression is the main hook, not immediate narrative payoff.

Local co-op is available for two players, and Steam Remote Play Together can be used for online play. This is a useful inclusion, but again it is presented as a convenience feature rather than a transformative mode. Likewise, the game advertises a vast, colorful fantasy world with hand-crafted areas, gorgeous art, and frame-by-frame animation. These visual claims may help the game stand out aesthetically, but the page relies on them so heavily that it feels like style is doing much of the heavy lifting.

Overall, Astral Ascent appears to be a polished but conventional roguelite platformer built on familiar promises: multiple heroes, randomized runs, build crafting, co-op, and escalating boss encounters. Its store page emphasizes variety and presentation, yet offers little that suggests it rises meaningfully above the crowded genre it clearly belongs to.
