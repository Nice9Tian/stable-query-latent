*Another Brick in The Mall* is a cold, micromanagement-heavy retail tycoon that reduces the fantasy of building a shopping center to a grim exercise in optimization and exploitation. You are tasked with designing, constructing, and managing a giant mall from the ground up, placing every road, parking space, wall, floor tile, door, shelf, and plant yourself. The game promises freedom, but that freedom is mostly a burden: every decision is another logistical problem to solve, another layout to tweak, another system to keep from collapsing.

The scope sounds impressive on paper. You can open shops, supermarkets, restaurants, movie theaters, bowling alleys, and more, then fill the place with thousands of simulated characters and vehicles for that supposed “ant colony feel.” In practice, this just means constant pressure to arrange everything as efficiently as possible, or watch the whole operation become a mess. Success depends on keeping queues short, shelves stocked, and floors clean, while juggling staff assignments and trying to prevent your mall from turning into an unhappy crowd simulation.

Hiring workers is handled through an applicant panel, where you’re expected to match skills to jobs, then deploy staff with ruthless efficiency. Customers are not people so much as revenue sources to be manipulated. You’re encouraged to sell staple goods to draw them in, then prey on their cravings and addictions to force impulse purchases. If you fail to keep them satisfied, they won’t return; if you really fail, they’ll complain loudly enough to damage your reputation. The whole system feels built around squeezing value out of everyone involved.

The financial side is equally unforgiving. You start with limited funds, so responsible accounting is essential if you want to survive long enough to expand. You can open your first business, try to turn a profit, take out loans, and gradually grow your center. There is also a sandbox mode with infinite money, but that only removes the one constraint that might make the experience bearable. Even then, the game’s identity remains the same: a dreary management simulator obsessed with control, profit, and overengineered retail sprawl.
