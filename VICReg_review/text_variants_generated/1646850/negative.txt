SpaceBourne 2 presents itself as a sprawling single-player open-universe RPG and third-person shooter, but the sheer density of its feature list feels more like a checklist than a cohesive vision. It promises role-playing, mining, trading, piracy, crafting, and deep exploration, alongside a story that continues from SpaceBourne and asks the player to build a new empire in the galaxy. In practice, the pitch is less about focused design and more about overwhelming scope.

The game advertises tens of thousands of procedurally generated solar systems and planets, each supposedly unique in structure, surface, climate, gravity, and points of interest. There are massive planetary surfaces, cities, villages, caves, outposts, and secrets to uncover, but the reliance on procedural generation raises the obvious concern that quantity may be substituted for memorable design. The galaxy sounds enormous, yet enormous is not the same as engaging.

SpaceBourne 2 also leans heavily into empire management and faction control. Players can establish their own faction, decide its internal operations, policies, governing principles, and management structure, then expand through military force, station capture, or new construction to become a ruler of the galaxy. On paper, that is ambitious. In reality, it reads like another layer of systems piled onto an already crowded foundation, where “complete control” risks becoming more bookkeeping than meaningful strategy.

Diplomacy is expanded through Starlords and Houses connected to each faction, each with their own characteristics, stations, planets, cities, and fleets. The game claims this structure can alter diplomatic dynamics dramatically, forcing players to think carefully before acting. That may sound impressive, but it also suggests a complicated web of relationships that could easily become opaque or tedious rather than truly deep.

The player is not left alone, at least in theory. Companions can assist in combat and lead the team, and members can be assigned different roles based on their skills. The game also includes a new character customization system and Unreal Engine rendering intended to create more lifelike characters, though visual realism does not automatically translate into a better experience. Likewise, the ability to modify ships according to personal preference sounds appealing, but it is still just another feature in an already overstuffed package.

Beyond the main structure, SpaceBourne 2 includes seven guilds that players can join and progress through, each offering side quests tied to its area of expertise and unique benefits as ranks increase. There are also side missions scattered across the galaxy, meant to provide extra rewards and world-building. A “living galaxy” rounds out the pitch, with communication between ships and random encounter events that are meant to create new stories and challenges. Yet all of this reinforces the same impression: the game wants to be everything at once, and that ambition may leave it feeling unfocused, bloated, and difficult to care about despite its massive scale.
