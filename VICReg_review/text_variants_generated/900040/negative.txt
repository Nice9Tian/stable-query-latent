ELEX II tries to sell itself as a grand return to Piranha Bytes’ post-apocalyptic science-fantasy formula, but it mostly comes across as a reheated continuation of the first game’s ideas. Set once again on Magalan, it follows Jax after he has already defeated the Hybrid, only to be dragged back into conflict when a new threat arrives from the sky and unleashes the dangerous power of dark Elex. The premise is simple enough: Jax must persuade the factions to unite, protect his family, and find his separated son, Dex. That sounds urgent, but the page frames it in a familiar open-world structure that seems more interested in promising scale than in offering anything truly fresh.

The game emphasizes “massive environments” and “unrivaled freedom,” relying heavily on a jetpack that lets players move across the map and even fly. It is clearly built around exploration, but the pitch feels generic: a huge handcrafted world, multiple factions, diverse environments, and a science-fantasy setting that blends apocalypse with pulp spectacle. The problem is that the description leans so hard on size and freedom that it gives the impression of a game more preoccupied with letting players roam than with giving that roaming much purpose.

Interaction with the world is presented as a major feature, though the details do not make it sound especially novel. NPCs are described as unique and reactive, remembering what the player has done and responding accordingly, which is standard open-world RPG framing at this point. Companions may join or leave depending on behavior, and they can even be killed, affecting the story. These systems suggest consequence, but the page offers no sign that they lead to anything beyond familiar branching reactions and moral bookkeeping. It reads like a checklist of RPG features rather than a convincing sign of depth.

Combat is another area where the game promises improvement, specifically fluid close and ranged combat with a “massively improved control system.” That wording implies the original needed serious fixing, and even here the description does little to inspire confidence beyond asserting that the controls are better. Like the rest of the page, it focuses on broad assurances instead of concrete excitement.

In the end, ELEX II appears to be a large, traditional open-world RPG that depends on exploration, factions, companion management, and consequence-driven choices. It is powered by the Piranha Bytes Engine Genome in its sixth iteration, which may matter to fans of the studio’s work, but the store text itself does not do much to make the sequel feel compelling. It promises freedom, scale, and reactive systems, yet the overall presentation feels tired, overfamiliar, and weighed down by the same old open-world ambitions.
