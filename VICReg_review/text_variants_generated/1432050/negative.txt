*Nobody Saves the World* presents itself as a quirky action RPG, but beneath the joke is a system built around grinding through transformations and ability combinations that often feels more clever on paper than satisfying in practice. You play as Nobody, a character tasked with stopping The Calamity after an ancient disaster reawakens, and the game’s central gimmick is its ever-expanding list of Forms. You can become a Slug, Ghost, Dragon, Rat, Rogue, Robot, Egg, and more, with over 15 distinct forms to unlock through quests.

The problem is that this variety can feel less like freedom and more like a maze of compulsory experimentation. Each Form comes with its own mechanics, its own quest line, and its own abilities, and the game constantly pushes you to mix and match more than 80 abilities to unlock new powers and progress further. That sounds flexible, but it also means the game is often more interested in making you juggle systems than letting any one of them breathe. Even the absurdity of turning into an Egg that leaves slime and shoots fireballs is delivered in a way that feels self-consciously whimsical rather than genuinely inventive.

Exploration happens across a large overworld, either solo or with a friend online, and the game also includes local and online co-op. Its dungeons are procedurally generated and supposedly evolve in difficulty and complexity as you grow stronger, but this progression can come across as a blunt escalation rather than a meaningful curve. The promise of “unconventional quests” and creative solutions is there, yet the structure still seems designed to keep nudging you through another checklist of tasks until the next form or upgrade unlocks.

There is also an infinite endgame dungeon, New Game+ with remixed difficulty modifiers, and a statue-building reward for those willing to keep going. But these features read more like content padding than compelling reasons to stay engaged. Even the soundtrack, composed by Jim Guthrie, is presented as a selling point in a game whose core loop appears to rely heavily on novelty for its own sake. *Nobody Saves the World* is certainly packed with ideas, but it leans so hard on constant reinvention that it risks becoming exhausting instead of exciting.
