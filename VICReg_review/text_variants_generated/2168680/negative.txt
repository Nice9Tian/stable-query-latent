*Nuclear Option* presents itself as a combat flight game, but what it really offers is a bleak spectacle of modern aerial warfare dressed up with technical jargon. Set on a “dynamic and realistic battlefield” of the near future, it throws players into messy engagements where intercepting bombers, dueling fighters, flying low under radar, and even using nuclear weapons are all treated as routine tools of destruction rather than meaningful strategic choices.

The game’s seven playable aircraft — ranging from the CI-22 “Cricket” counter-insurgency plane to the SFB-81 “Darkreach” strike bomber — are each paired with scenarios meant to showcase their supposed strengths. In practice, this just sounds like a checklist of military roles: light attack, stealth attack helicopter, air superiority fighter, multirole fighter, electronic warfare craft, and bomber. The result is a broad but grim set of machines built for one thing only: escalating damage. There is little indication of depth beyond the surface variety of hardware.

Its flight model is described as “realistic,” with stalls, spins, g-force effects, dozens of aerodynamic surfaces, and aircraft made from up to 50 simulated physics parts. The game also leans heavily on detailed damage modeling, where the condition of individual parts affects flight behavior. That may sound impressive, but it also suggests a punishing experience where complexity is piled on for its own sake. Even basic control is framed as something that needs accommodation, since the game offers a mouse-controlled “virtual joystick” for those without special hardware. This is not exactly an inviting setup.

Cockpits are packed with HUDs, target indicators, threat readouts, and flight parameters such as angle of attack, velocity vector, climb rate, G force, and radar altitude. Instead of creating tension or immersion, the interface sounds overloaded, as if the game is trying too hard to impress with information. Night missions and night-vision cockpit functionality add another layer of operational obscurity, but not necessarily clarity or excitement.

Combat itself is built around a long list of weapons: guns, air-to-air missiles, air-to-ground missiles, bombs, cruise missiles, ballistic missiles, countermeasures, and nuclear arms with simulated blast and shockwave propagation. The inclusion of nuclear weapons is particularly tone-deaf, presented as just another battlefield option. The game’s persistent battlegrounds, wreckage, smoke, AI aircraft, and ground vehicles contribute to a constantly active war zone, but also a sense of relentless destruction with no relief.

Multiplayer supports player-hosted sessions with public lobbies and both cooperative and competitive play, while the mission editor lets players build their own scenarios. There is a 100km-wide map with multiple biomes and terrain types, which sounds large but also potentially empty given the focus on open-ended combat. Overall, *Nuclear Option* seems less like a polished flight experience and more like a dense military simulation obsessed with scale, damage, and devastation. It promises realism, but what comes through most clearly is a cold, exhausting battlefield simulator that mistakes complexity for appeal.
