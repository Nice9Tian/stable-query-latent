WWE 2K24 leans heavily on spectacle and nostalgia, but the store page makes it hard to ignore how much of the package is built around selling variations of the same content. The headline WrestleMania celebration sounds grand, yet much of the appeal is essentially a pile of editions, bonus packs, currency, and unlocks layered on top of a standard wrestling game.

The core promise is “Forty Years of WrestleMania,” presented through 2K Showcase of the Immortals, where players relive selected career-defining matches and iconic moments. That sounds like the game’s main historical hook, but the rest of the page suggests a product more interested in content segmentation than a clean, complete experience. Different editions bolt on extra attires, MyFACTION cards, VC, and DLC packs, making the whole offering feel fragmented and aggressively monetized.

The roster is admittedly large, mixing legends like “Stone Cold” Steve Austin, Undertaker, and Andre the Giant with modern stars such as Cody Rhodes, John Cena, Rhea Ripley, and Roman Reigns. Still, the page emphasizes their “ultra-realistic graphics” and entrances more than any deeper gameplay distinction, which gives the impression of presentation carrying a lot of the weight.

The feature list is broad, but not especially surprising for the genre. WWE 2K24 includes new match types such as Guest Referee, Casket, Gauntlet, and Ambulance matches, plus support for multiple Superstars in Backstage Brawl. MyRISE offers two career arcs with original storylines for men’s and women’s divisions, while MyFACTION revolves around card collection, online multiplayer, ranked quickplay, and seasonal rewards. Universe mode continues as the sandbox for feuds and weekly shows, and MyGM lets players manage brands, contracts, trades, and rival general managers. None of these modes sound bad on paper, but the page presents them in a way that feels more like a checklist than a compelling vision.

The edition structure is especially bloated. The Bray Wyatt Edition, Forty Years of WrestleMania Edition, Deluxe Edition, Bray Wyatt Bundle, and standard game all overlap in ways that make the storefront feel overcomplicated. Many of the extras are limited to alternate attires, MyFACTION cards, manager cards, championship belts, or virtual currency, which hardly justifies the premium packaging. Even the Bray Wyatt content is framed as an “exclusive release” celebration, but it mostly amounts to another bundle of cosmetics, cards, and VC alongside a playable version of The Fiend.

There is some fan-service value here for dedicated WWE followers, particularly those interested in WrestleMania history or Bray Wyatt memorabilia. But the page sells the game in a way that feels more cynical than celebratory: every feature arrives wrapped in a monetized edition, every bonus is split across tiers, and even unlocks are turned into selling points. WWE 2K24 may have a large roster and a wide feature set, but the store page makes it look less like a unified wrestling experience and more like a carefully segmented storefront built around extras.
