*Startup Company* is a business simulation sandbox that sounds ambitious on paper but quickly reveals itself as a fairly relentless exercise in micromanagement. You play as the CEO of a small startup with the vague dream of becoming the most valuable company in history, but the path there is mostly a grind of continuously developing website features, expanding hosting capacity, and running marketing campaigns.

The game centers on managing a website rather than building something especially creative or exciting. Your success depends on keeping the site functional and growing, which means dealing with servers, traffic, and the endless pressure to improve. At first, you rely on virtual servers, but as the website attracts more users, you’re forced into building your own hosting center. It is the kind of escalation that sounds impressive, yet mostly amounts to a larger, more complicated set of problems.

Office management is another layer of obligation. The city offers offices in different sizes and shapes, and you have to choose one, organize it, decorate it, and somehow keep employees satisfied while doing so. That’s easier said than done, because your workers are treated as vital assets rather than people. As the company grows, you must recruit more staff, only to have them demand more over time. If you fail to keep them happy, competitors may poach them with offers they “can’t reject,” which adds yet another annoyance to an already stressful loop.

The competitive side is equally mechanical. The stated goal is to become the biggest website in the industry, either by buying and merging with competitors or by simply outlasting them until they fail. It’s a corporate fantasy built on familiar business-sim routines, but there’s little in the description to suggest anything more than standard management obligations stacked one after another.

At least the game supports modding through the Steam Workshop, and it also encourages community translation through Localizor. That may be the most appealing part, since the core premise itself is mostly a checklist of startup clichés, employee management chores, and server maintenance dressed up as a dream of tech-world dominance.
