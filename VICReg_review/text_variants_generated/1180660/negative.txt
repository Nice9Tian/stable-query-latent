Tell Me Why presents itself as a narrative adventure from DONTNOD Entertainment, the studio behind Life is Strange, but beneath that familiar label it mostly offers the same kind of slow, choice-driven drama with a supernatural wrapper. The game follows reunited twins Tyler and Alyson Ronan as they use their “supernatural bond” to dig through conflicting memories of a troubled childhood. Set in a picturesque small-town Alaska, it leans heavily on atmosphere and emotional framing, but the actual premise is still built around revisiting the past, piecing together fragments of truth, and making decisions that supposedly shape the twins’ bond and future.

The game advertises “true-to-life characters,” mature themes, and “gripping choices,” yet that promise seems to rest on a very familiar narrative-adventure formula rather than anything especially bold. Players are expected to uncover the truth by reliving memories, influence the relationship between the twins through branching choices, and solve puzzles tied to a fantasy element called The Book of Goblins. The result sounds less like a fresh mystery and more like a carefully constructed emotional puzzle box, one that depends on guided revelations and binary-feeling decisions to create the illusion of depth.

Its setting is another major selling point, with “beautiful, highly detailed small-town Alaska” presented in 4K and HDR, but visual polish does not necessarily compensate for the game’s narrow structure. The emphasis remains on walking through locations, uncovering secrets, and watching the story react to selected choices. Even the inclusion of puzzles appears designed more to pace the narrative than to challenge the player in any meaningful way.

The game also highlights that it was developed with research and consultation from cultural, mental health, and gender advocates, which suggests an attempt at sensitivity and authenticity. Still, that kind of framing can’t hide the fact that Tell Me Why is fundamentally a heavily scripted interactive drama that asks players to accept its emotional weight while offering limited agency. It is a polished, earnest, and carefully researched story experience, but one that seems content to recycle the genre’s usual dependence on mood, memory, and consequence without delivering much beyond that.
