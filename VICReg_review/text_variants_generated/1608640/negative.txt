OTXO is a violent top-down shooter with roguelite elements, but despite that premise, it seems built around a grim loop of repetition rather than genuine momentum. You play as a nameless protagonist who enters an inexplicable mansion to search for a lost love, slowly uncovering the mansion’s secrets while pushing through its many hostile areas. The setup aims for mystery and emotional urgency, yet the game leans so heavily on brutality and bleakness that the narrative framework feels more like a thin excuse for constant violence.

Gameplay revolves around cutting through enemies with a massive variety of guns, over 100 abilities, and a slow-motion Focus mechanic used to dodge bullets. In theory, this should create a sense of power and control, but the combat also depends on adapting to a variety of enemy types that can quickly turn the tables if you fail to react properly. That tension may sound exciting, but it also suggests a familiar roguelite strain of punishing escalation, where success is less about discovery than surviving another wave of threats.

Replayability is clearly one of the game’s central claims, with 8 areas and more than 150 hand-crafted rooms arranged randomly so that no two runs are the same. Still, this kind of structure can easily become exhausting when the game’s identity is built on replaying brutal encounters inside a mansion full of secrets that are only gradually revealed. The promise of variety does not necessarily mean variety in experience, and the repeated cycle may feel more mechanical than meaningful.

Customization is offered through unlockable weapons and acquirable abilities from the bartender, letting players search for a setup that suits their preferred approach. Yet this sounds less like deep strategic freedom and more like another layer of grindable optimization in service of the same violent loop. Even the soundtrack, described as a gritty, pulse-pumping noir-style murder ballet composed by the developer, reinforces the game’s commitment to oppressive style over warmth or relief.

Overall, OTXO presents itself as an intense, stylish roguelite shooter, but its own features point to a relentlessly harsh and repetitive design. The mansion, the mystery, the lost love, and the inner demons all exist inside a game that seems determined to reduce everything to blood, bullets, and attrition.
