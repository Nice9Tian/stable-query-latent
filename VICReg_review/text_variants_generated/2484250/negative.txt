ASTRA: Knights of Veda presents itself as a dark medieval fantasy, but what it really offers is a familiar coat of grim atmosphere wrapped around a bleak struggle for survival. The continent of Planis is already in ruin under the tyranny of Mad King Magnus, and the story wastes no time piling on more misery: a war to free the people ends in catastrophe when a giant tree of death rises and twists the living into monsters. From there, the goddess Veda selects a new champion, and the player becomes the “Master of the Book,” joining the Knights of Veda in yet another desperate attempt to restore order to a world teetering on oblivion.

The game pushes players through this cursed setting with exploration and combat framed as a side-scrolling action adventure. You travel across Planis, facing endless undead and beasts in the Nightmare, while tougher bosses wait in Sealed Prisons. The structure sounds harsh because it is meant to be: stand alone, fight monstrosities, and earn rewards to strengthen your Knights of Veda. That loop is straightforward, but also rather unforgiving in tone, with the game repeatedly emphasizing fatal challenges and overwhelming threats rather than any sense of freedom or hope.

Combat is described as tactical and stylish 2D action, where a single skill, a dodge at the right moment, or a burst of attacks can decide the outcome. The game leans heavily on timing and on the powers of the Knights of Veda, along with the so-called Overflowing Power of the Stars, to overcome enemies. It clearly wants to evoke the old golden age of 2D side-scrolling action games, though that ambition is filtered through a grim fantasy setting that feels more oppressive than exciting.

Visually, the game relies on dark, elaborate artwork to sell its world. The Knights, relics, and bosses are presented as carefully designed pieces of a fantasy universe, but the page’s emphasis on “stunning artwork” cannot hide the fact that the atmosphere is built on death, corruption, and intimidation. Even the story presentation is tied to this heavy mood, with expansive cutscenes meant to unfold the journey in dramatic fashion.

In the end, ASTRA: Knights of Veda appears to be a bleak action RPG built around grim lore, tough encounters, and stylized 2D combat. It promises mystery and allure, but the dominant impression is one of despair and relentless conflict, with little beyond spectacle and punishment to keep the world of Planis moving forward.
