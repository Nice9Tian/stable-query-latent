Exoprimal is an online-only team-based action game that sounds more inventive than it often feels on paper. Its central hook is simple enough: humanity’s exosuit technology versus waves of dinosaurs. In practice, that means two squads of five throwing themselves into objective-based matches while a hyper-advanced AI, Leviathan, dictates the pace of the chaos. The premise leans heavily on spectacle, but the structure is built around repetition, mission completion, and constant team coordination rather than any deeper strategic elegance.

Set in the year 2040, the story opens with sudden dinosaur outbreaks threatening humanity’s survival. The corporation Aibius claims to have a solution in the form of Leviathan, an AI that can predict future outbreak locations, and in the exosuits that give pilots a fighting chance. Players become exofighters after taking an aptitude test, entering Aibius’s ranks as pilots tasked with pushing back extinction. It is a standard crisis setup dressed in sci-fi jargon, with the dinosaurs treated less as mysterious creatures and more as an endless disaster to be managed.

The game’s exosuit system is its main mechanical foundation, but even that is framed in a fairly rigid way. Exosuits are divided into three roles: assault, tank, and support. Assault suits are built to deal damage at close, medium, or long range. Tank suits are meant to absorb attacks and protect allies. Support suits focus on survival tools such as repairing allies, buffing teammates, and debuffing enemies. Players can change exosuits at any time, even during battle, which gives the system some flexibility, but the game still pushes everyone into clearly defined team functions. It is less about creative freedom than about filling a role and hoping the team composition holds together.

Rigs add another layer of equipment, but they also seem designed to patch over the weaknesses of a chosen exosuit rather than transform the experience. Only one rig can be equipped at a time, and they grant extra abilities. The examples given make the system sound like a compensatory tool: a close-range suit can get a long-range laser, or a front-line defender can gain healing. That flexibility is welcome, but it also suggests a game that is constantly trying to balance its own shortcomings through modular add-ons instead of giving players more genuinely distinct choices. Still, both rigs and exosuits can be changed at any time, so the game keeps nudging players to adapt on the fly.

The main mode described is Dino Survival, where two squads of five race to complete objectives before the opposing team. Sometimes the squads fight each other directly, and sometimes they are forced to cooperate against a major foe. Missions vary depending on progression, so matches are not always identical, even when the surroundings and objective structure feel familiar. Playing this mode unlocks story sequences and rewards experience points for both player and exosuit levels. That progression loop is familiar and functional, but not especially exciting; it relies on grinding through matches to advance rather than on memorable standalone encounters.

The game’s enemies are dinosaurs that have been distorted by travel through spacetime vortexes, with Neosaurs representing especially unstable mutations. These creatures are described as having unusual appearances, abilities, and attacks due to an intense concentration of unstable energy. It is a flashy explanation for why the dinosaurs are more dangerous than normal, but it does not add much depth beyond escalating the threat level. The result is a premise built on constant escalation: more outbreaks, more mutations, more suits, more mission churn.

The store page also makes clear that Exoprimal has a layered product structure, including a Deluxe Edition with a Head Start Kit, early unlock tickets for specific exosuits, and cosmetic skins such as Paladin, White Guardian, and Wonderland. The page explicitly notes that the skins are cosmetic only and that the Head Start Kit can be purchased separately. That commercial framing feels very much in step with the rest of the game: a service-like online action title with progression, unlocks, and extra purchases arranged around the core loop.

Overall, Exoprimal presents a loud, team-based dinosaur shooter built on role assignment, mission repetition, and matchmaking-driven chaos. It has a strong pitch in theory, but the available details suggest a game more concerned with functional structure and monetized progression than with anything truly distinctive.
