League of Legends is a multiplayer online battle arena game from Riot Games, and despite its enormous popularity, its core design remains largely tied to a familiar and derivative formula. It was inspired by Defense of the Ancients, and Riot’s founders set out to make a standalone game in the same genre rather than introduce anything especially groundbreaking. The result is a MOBA where players control a champion from an isometric perspective, using a small set of unique abilities to fight on maps like Summoner’s Rift.

The basic progression is straightforward and repetitive. Champions gain levels by earning experience points through killing enemies, while gold is collected passively over time and actively through defeating enemy minions, champions, and defensive structures. That gold is then spent on items to increase a champion’s strength. In practice, the game revolves around this cycle of farming, fighting, and purchasing upgrades, which can feel more like a rigid economic routine than an especially inspired battlefield experience.

The game’s original narrative framing was also thin. Before 2014, players were presented as political leaders, or “Summoners,” commanding champions in the Fields of Justice to prevent a catastrophic war, such as on Summoner’s Rift. However, even this setup was criticized for lacking depth. Sociologist Matt Watson noted that the plot and setting were stripped of the political themes found in other role-playing games and instead reduced to simplistic “good versus evil” terms. Early development did little to improve this weakness, since Riot did not initially hire writers and character biographies were only a paragraph long.

Overall, League of Legends is built around competitive mechanics and champion-based combat, but its worldbuilding and storytelling are notably shallow. Its systems are functional, yet the game’s premise, progression, and early lore presentation all come across as limited and reductive.
