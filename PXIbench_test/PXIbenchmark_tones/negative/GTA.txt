Grand Theft Auto is a crude and intentionally chaotic 1997 action-adventure game that serves as the first entry in the long-running series, but even in its original form it feels more like a harsh, uneven prototype than a polished crime epic. Developed by DMA Design and published by BMG Interactive, it was released across MS-DOS, Windows, PlayStation, and later Game Boy Color, yet its structure remains rigid and limited: six levels divided among three fictionalized U.S. cities—Liberty City, San Andreas, and Vice City—each borrowing heavily from New York City, San Francisco, and Miami.

The game’s progression is strictly linear, with each completed level simply unlocking the next, leaving little room for real freedom in structure despite the open criminal theme. Players start by choosing one of eight characters, or four in the PlayStation version, and naming them, but this choice is purely cosmetic and has no effect on gameplay. That empty selection reflects a broader weakness in the game’s design: the surface suggests variation, but the mechanics do not support meaningful individuality.

The story is equally blunt. The protagonist begins in Liberty City working for Robert “Bubby” Seragliano’s gang, taking on job after job until a rival gang warns that these actions are badly damaging Seragliano’s boss, Sonetti. Rather than building tension with nuance, the game pushes the player onward through a straightforward chain of tasks, and the protagonist simply continues serving Seragliano before eventually killing Sonetti. It is a grim, unsentimental setup that fits the game’s violent tone, but it offers little emotional depth or sophistication.

As a result, Grand Theft Auto stands as an early and rather clumsy foundation for the series: visually and conceptually bold in places, but mechanically restrictive, narratively blunt, and ultimately far less refined than its reputation might suggest.
