Overwatch is a multimedia franchise built around a team-based first-person shooter that has always leaned heavily on spectacle over substance. Developed by Blizzard Entertainment, the series centers on hero shooters where players pick from a large roster of characters—52 by Season 2—split into Tank, Damage, and Support roles. Tanks are bulkier and meant to absorb pressure and protect teammates, Damage heroes are the main offensive force, and Support characters are supposed to heal, buff allies, or weaken enemies. In theory, this creates varied team play; in practice, it is another layered system that only works if everyone does their job, which is often a poor assumption in multiplayer games.

The original Overwatch launched in 2016, but it notably lacked any traditional story mode, leaving its narrative ambitions scattered across comics, literary tie-ins, animated shorts, and other transmedia material. Blizzard clearly preferred to outsource its world-building to supplementary media rather than integrate it into the game itself. Overwatch 2 followed in 2022, and the original game was taken offline, only to have Blizzard rename the series back to Overwatch in 2026, adding yet another awkward bit of branding confusion to the franchise’s history.

Beyond the matches themselves, Blizzard also pushed hard to turn Overwatch into an esports product. That included the Overwatch World Cup, the Overwatch League, a minor league structure, and later the Overwatch Champions Series, which borrowed elements from traditional American sports leagues. The result is a franchise that has always seemed more interested in packaging itself as a competitive spectacle and multimedia brand than in offering a straightforward, satisfying game experience.
