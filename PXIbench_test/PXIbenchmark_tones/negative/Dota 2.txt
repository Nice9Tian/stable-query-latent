Dota 2 is a 2013 MOBA from Valve that feels more like a demanding legacy project than a cleanly welcoming game. Built as a sequel to Defense of the Ancients, it inherits the same basic premise: two teams of five fight to destroy the enemy Ancient while trying to protect their own. That structure is clear enough, but the game’s reliance on standard real-time strategy controls and a single isometric map makes it seem stubbornly rooted in older design conventions rather than anything genuinely approachable.

The roster is enormous, with 127 playable heroes, each supposedly defined by their own strengths and weaknesses. In practice, that sheer scale can come across as overwhelming and needlessly complicated, especially for anyone hoping for a straightforward competitive experience. The game certainly offers variety, but much of it is buried under a steep learning curve and the pressure of team coordination.

Dota 2 also leans heavily on its esports identity, with professional leagues and tournaments drawing teams from around the world. Yet this competitive focus can make the game feel less like a fun strategy title and more like an exhausting test of knowledge, reflexes, and endurance. Valve has added several special modes over time, including a ten-versus-ten mode, the Halloween-themed Colosseum capture point mode, the combat arena mode Overthrow, the cooperative campaign Siltbreaker, and The Underhollow battle royale mode. These additions do broaden the package, but they also underline how much the core experience needed extra modes to keep itself interesting.

As a sequel to a mod that had already become a major esports phenomenon by the late 2000s, Dota 2 carries a lot of historical weight. Unfortunately, that heritage also leaves it feeling burdened by complexity and tradition, making it a game that seems impressive on paper but intimidating and unforgiving in actual play.
