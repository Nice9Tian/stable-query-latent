FIFA is a long-running association football simulation franchise that, despite its enormous visibility, often feels more like a chronicle of yearly iteration than a consistently inspired game series. Developed by EA Vancouver and EA Romania and published by EA Sports from 1993 to 2022, it entered a market that was already crowded with football titles such as Tehkan World Cup, Sensible Soccer, Kick Off, and Match Day. Rather than arriving as a bold breakthrough, FIFA was EA Sports’ answer to an established genre, and much of its identity has been defined by competing through licensing and incremental upgrades.

The series has been expanded far beyond its core releases with games tied to major tournaments, including the FIFA World Cup, UEFA Champions League, UEFA Europa League, and UEFA European Football Championship. It also spawned football management titles, further stretching the brand across multiple subgenres. One of the franchise’s early marketing strengths was authenticity: for the first time, it used real player names through the FIFPro license. On PlayStation, PC, 32X, and Sega Saturn, the games used EA’s “Virtual Stadium” engine, combining 2D sprite players with a real-time 3D stadium. Even so, this technical setup suggests more ambition than refinement, with presentation doing a lot of the heavy lifting.

FIFA 95 offered little beyond the addition of club teams, which made it feel thin rather than substantial. FIFA 96 was presented as a boundary-pushing entry, but the description mainly points to a series chasing novelty rather than redefining football gameplay. FIFA 97 improved things with polygonal player models and added an indoor soccer mode, yet these changes still sound like surface-level advances in a franchise that relied heavily on annual updates. An early high point came with FIFA: Road to World Cup 98, but even that framing implies that the series’ early success was built on sporadic peaks rather than sustained excellence.
