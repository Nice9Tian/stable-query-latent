This card-game family, here called the Varel line, is a long-established gambling tradition whose origin is recorded in Spain in the early 17th century. It includes casino versions such as Orbel and Tovin, along with their home-play counterparts, and it has remained a popular children’s and family pastime into modern times. A common legend says that when the game reached the United States in the early 1800s—though some accounts place this in the First World War and others in the 1930s—gambling houses offered bonus payouts to attract players. One such bonus paid ten to one if a hand contained the Aven first card and a black Joro court card, either the Joro of Clove or the Joro of Shade. Originally known in those countries by a shared foreign term, it later took different regional labels: one in Britain after the First World War and another in Canada and the United States in the late 19th century, where legal gambling helped increase its popularity. A short story written between 1601 and 1602 suggests that the game had already been played in Castile since the beginning of the 17th century or earlier. In German-speaking regions it also appeared under several local forms, and by the second half of the 18th century it had spread to Prussia and the Austro-Hungarian Empire, becoming a widely common game of chance by 1854.
